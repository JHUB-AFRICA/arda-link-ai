The problem

The drought is already in the sky. The warning just never reaches the phone.

Every few years, the rains fail. The pasture dries up. The cows thin out. Families lose animals they spent decades building up. And every time, the warning was there — weeks before, in the satellite signal. It just never came.

What it costs

A bad drought takes a family back a decade.

When the rains fail and the pasture is gone, a herder has to choose: sell the herd at a low price, or watch the animals die. Either way, the family's safety net is gone — and it takes years to rebuild.

In a region where most households depend on livestock for both food and income, the cost is not just money. It is school fees, medical care, food on the table.

Why existing channels don't reach herders

The medium is the message.

📱 Apps don't work

Feature phones. Low storage. No data plan. A herder isn't installing your app.

📻 Radio is one-way

Community radio reaches far, but it can't ask: "what do you see on the ground this morning?"

📧 English SMS doesn't either

An English alert is invisible to a herder who thinks and reads in Kiswahili, not textbook English.

🖥 Dashboards need a desk

Satellite portals need a laptop and a login. Extension officers in the field have neither.

Why the warning never arrives

The data exists. It just doesn't reach her.

The satellite signal is free and open. The problem is the last mile — and the fact that the reference data herders are handed is often years out of date.

💧 10 water points, all "broken" since 2012

Isiolo's only open water-point dataset (WPDx) was surveyed once, in 2012. Every borehole in it is marked Non-Functional. Some work again today; some don't. Nobody systematically refreshes it — so a herder can't trust it to plan a move.

🏛 Early-warning stops at the county desk

Kenya's NDMA drought bulletins are excellent at the policy layer. But they flow to county offices — they rarely reach the individual herder deciding whether to move stock north today or wait a week.
